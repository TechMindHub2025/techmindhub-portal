Who Monitors the AI Monitor? Lessons from an External OpenAI Review

An AI monitor can flag suspicious behaviour only if the relevant evidence reaches it. That makes the collection and routing of evidence part of the safety argument.

METR and Redwood Research published a high-access pilot review on October 22. The exercise examined a historical OpenAI monitoring system. It identified coverage and safeguard weaknesses; the evaluators did not verify OpenAI's subsequent statements about production fixes. Possible bypasses are not evidence of actual exploitation by deployed agents.

For an engineering team, our assessment is that three questions deserve separate answers:

Coverage: do the actions and context that matter actually arrive for review?

Detection: what evidence shows that the monitor recognises relevant risks, including cases that nobody has already reported?

Response: can a responsible person intervene before the underlying action becomes difficult or impossible to reverse?

Those questions lead to different acceptance tests. A system can interpret the records it receives well while missing an execution route. It can also raise a correct alert after the opportunity to prevent harm has passed.

Our suggested starting point is one narrowly defined workflow. Trace representative test actions through execution, logging, review and intervention. Record what remains outside that test before expanding the agent's permissions.

A monitor cannot assess evidence it never receives. What evidence would convince you that your own agent's monitoring is complete enough for its permissions?
