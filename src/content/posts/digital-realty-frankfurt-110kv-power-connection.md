The first constraint on a large AI campus may be outside its buildings. Digital Realty [announced on November 17](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/11/17/3404295/0/en/digital-realty-supports-growth-at-digital-park-fechenheim-with-dedicated-110-kv-power-connection-now-online.html) that a dedicated **110 kV underground power connection** from the Karben substation to Digital Park Fechenheim in Frankfurt is now online. The route is approximately **16 km**, and the connection is designed for up to **300 MVA**.

Digital Realty says construction and commissioning took about 20 months, with investment in the triple-digit millions of euros. More than 70% of the route used trenchless construction. The company owns the connection, according to its release. These details make the project a useful illustration of how securing data-center capacity can involve substantial civil and grid engineering well before an additional server hall is ready.

## The cable is a campus-scale commitment

Fechenheim currently has three operating data centers. A fourth is planned for 2027; the full campus plan calls for **11 data centers and up to 200 MW of IT capacity**. These are planned endpoints, not a statement that all 11 facilities or 200 MW are operating today. The date of the physical energisation is not separately specified in the announcement, even though the connection was reported as online on November 17.

An underground 110 kV route has its own schedule and risk profile: rights of way, crossings, civil works, terminations, protection coordination and commissioning all have to fit together. Trenchless installation can avoid opening the entire route, but it does not remove the need to validate cable thermal conditions, joints, switching arrangements and maintenance access. The press release provides the overall route and delivery figures, not a full electrical single-line diagram or independently measured availability.

## Why 300 MVA does not mean 300 MW of IT

**MVA is apparent-power capacity at the electrical connection; MW is real power.** Their relationship depends on power factor. More important here, a campus also uses power for cooling, UPS losses and other facility systems. A design-rated grid connection is neither the current load nor a direct conversion to the eventual IT nameplate. One cannot calculate current utilization, operating PUE or spare capacity merely by subtracting the stated 200 MW IT plan from 300 MVA.

The planning question is how much deliverable real power remains under specified power factor, redundancy, transformer configuration, cooling demand and permissible contingency operation. A dedicated feeder also does not by itself prove independent redundant utility paths. An engineering assessment would need the actual topology, protection zones, failure scenarios and power-quality requirements before making that claim.

The larger lesson is temporal: a campus may need to secure a high-voltage corridor and commission its connection while most of its eventual IT estate is still in the future. This project documents an operating piece of that infrastructure. The full campus expansion and its measured operating profile remain separate questions.
