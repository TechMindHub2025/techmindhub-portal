An electric vehicle battery is a performance choice and a procurement exposure. A [Nature Communications study published November 10](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-79046-y) asks whether changing battery chemistry and recycling material could soften the effect of lithium and cobalt disruptions on the US vehicle market. The paper was accepted October 8; the date of the underlying modelling work is not specified.

The researchers combined industry interviews, scenarios for technology and recycling by 2030, and an economic equilibrium model of automotive supply. They considered, among other cases, a year-long 30% restriction of lithium supply from China and the closure of three cobalt mines. In their model these shocks could raise the average price of **all new vehicles**, not just EVs, by about $1,100–$1,600 and impose $14–$17 billion in consumer costs. These are scenario outputs, not observed market prices or forecasts.

## Two different levers

Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) batteries avoid cobalt and nickel in the cathode, changing the mix of minerals that constrains production. Recycling provides a secondary material stream, but only if collection, processing capacity, recovery yields and timing can supply the needed material. Neither measure eliminates all lithium dependence.

In one combined scenario, 85% of battery-electric vehicle models shift to LFP and at least 100 kilotonnes of planned recycled lithium carbonate equivalent capacity becomes available. The model finds disruption costs fall by more than half. That result is **conditional on both assumptions**. The illustration shows the two levers separately so it does not imply that recycling or chemistry alone delivers the entire effect.

## From scenario to industrial decision

An automaker would need to test vehicle requirements, charging and range trade-offs, qualification time, contracts, refinery geography and recycling ramp schedules. A recycler's nameplate capacity is not necessarily recovered material delivered at specification. Substitution can also shift bottlenecks to other components or regions.

The paper gives a useful way to reason about resilience as a system property, rather than a single mine or chemistry. Its quantitative outcomes depend on the chosen shock, adoption rates and economic model. They should guide sensitivity analysis, not be reported as a guaranteed saving for a factory.
