Data centre efficiency is often expressed through electricity use. A cooling decision also commits the operator to materials that must be procured, maintained, recovered and eventually handled as waste. Those flows deserve their own record.

A [review published October 26, 2026, in Nature Reviews Clean Technology](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44359-026-00232-8) examines perfluoroalkyl and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) across data centre infrastructure, from semiconductor manufacturing through cooling to disposal and recycling. The authors map potential release pathways: manufacturing waste, coolant evaporation and leakage, and residual substances during hardware recycling. They consider capture, destruction, reduction and substitution, and identify gaps in application-specific flow data.

The review is a synthesis of existing evidence, not a demonstration that every facility uses the same fluid or that one replacement works for every installation. Its treatment options vary with waste concentration and physical form. Substitution must meet performance, safety and compatibility requirements. The authors also discuss computing efficiency, equipment life and reducing coolant inventories and losses as ways to reduce demand for these materials.

Our assessment is to widen the definition of infrastructure efficiency. A cooling design may reduce operating electricity while introducing material requirements that need separate accounting. Comparing systems means looking at both over their useful lives, rather than assuming that either measurement stands for the whole environmental outcome.

## Start with a balance, not an emissions claim

For a facility operator, the first practical step is a materials ledger for each relevant fluid and system. Record how much entered the installation, what remains in service, additions during maintenance, recovered amounts, and transfers to reuse or waste treatment. Keep the dates, measurement methods, units and responsible parties with each entry. Inventory records can be reconciled with purchase and service documentation.

An unexplained difference deserves investigation. It may reflect measurement error, unrecorded maintenance, residual fluid in equipment or an actual loss. The difference alone is not a measured environmental release. Treating it as such would confuse an accounting gap with an observed pathway.

The same discipline applies to the review's broader supply chain. A facility ledger can track its own cooling inventory, but it cannot directly measure PFAS used in upstream semiconductor production. Supplier disclosures, component documentation and waste-contractor records have different boundaries and levels of certainty. State them separately when comparing alternatives.

## Test substitutes as part of a system

A candidate replacement fluid must work with seals, tubing, component materials, operating temperature, thermal load and maintenance procedures. Fault conditions and service access matter as much as normal operation. A chemical datasheet can support screening; it cannot establish complete-system reliability. A controlled trial should define performance, safety and compatibility criteria before any wider change.

End-of-life management begins before retirement. Collecting spent fluid, transferring it to a contractor and verifying final treatment are separate milestones. The handover record should identify the material, quantity, receiver, treatment route and evidence returned. A chain of custody can expose where responsibility becomes unclear; it does not by itself prove the effectiveness of the final treatment.

The review's data gaps limit sector-wide comparisons. Estimated coolant stocks and assumed equipment populations should be distinguished from measured facility inventories and observed losses. More consistent records would help operators assess whether reducing losses, recovering fluid or extending equipment life offers a better result than an isolated chemical substitution.

The prospect is infrastructure planning that considers energy, materials and service life together. A good ledger will not solve every PFAS question, but it can make a facility's own decisions and uncertainties visible enough to test.
