An AI data center can be ready to order servers long before the electrical system is ready to deliver its load. That mismatch is now a planning problem for the regional grid, not merely a delay in an individual building project. On November 16, PJM [filed proposed tariff revisions](https://www.pjm.com/-/media/DotCom/documents/ferc/filings/2026/20261116-er27-531-000.pdf) with the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission in docket **ER27-531-000**. The filing introduces a Fast Energization Process and new transmission-service arrangements for large loads, including requests of **50 MW or more**.

## What the proposal changes

PJM seeks to coordinate the study of new generation, transmission and large demand more directly. A data center's desired energization date is not enough: the study must establish what network upgrades, generation resources and operating conditions can support the load. The proposed framework distinguishes **interim, firm and nonfirm** service, which can carry different reliability and curtailment implications. The choice of service is therefore an architectural and commercial decision for the customer, not a synonym for a faster permanent connection.

The filing contemplates a first full cycle that would conclude in **2029**. Its proposed study cadence should be read as a process target, not a promise that a particular campus will have power by a particular date. Even if a study runs faster, a substation, line or generating plant may still require construction, permitting and commissioning. Likewise, allocating upgrade costs affects who bears the price of connecting a large load; it does not remove the physical work.

## The engineering test

Operators should separate three questions in their plans: **when can equipment be energized, how firm is the service, and who pays for necessary upgrades?** Flexible operation may bridge some timing gaps, but only if a workload can actually curtail or shift without breaching customer and safety obligations. Backup generation, storage or demand management have their own permitting, emissions and duration limits. These choices need site-specific modeling rather than an assumption that any large data center is a dispatchable load.

This is a **regulatory filing, not an approved rule**. FERC may revise or reject provisions, and the eventual operational results remain unknown. The value of the proposal is the sharper coupling it attempts between compute expansion and electric-system evidence: a queue position is not a megawatt at the rack.
